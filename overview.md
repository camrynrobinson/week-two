I clicked on this video: ["Monsters Hiding Inside Your Games..." By Sagan Hawkes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lgpdqiJ50qs&t=26s) and he starts with this incredible quote that immediately got my brain firing:
> "The domain of myth and legend is not one limited to our physical world. Video games, too, are filled with folklore and mystery. From playground rumours to genuine anomalies and Easter eggs only proven or discovered years later. if you're supernaturally inclined, the virtual world has just as many opportunities for ghost hunting or cryptozoology as the woods of the Pacific Northwest or the mountains of Appalachia" 
> 
***YES!!!!!!!***
I got so excited about this that i didn't even watch the rest of it. just the idea of internet mythologies alone was so exciting that i had to write it down. i was one of those weird kids in middle school who loved creepypastas and cryptozoology, and engaged those interests purely over the internet. I had a particular fascination with "found footage"; there was this show i used to watch on YouTube called Lost Tapes or something like that. 
I can't believe it took me this long to realize that those internet horror stories (both the ones based in games and the ones that exist on their own) that are so ubiquitous online form a body digital folklore which can be studied!!! That's so cool!!!! 

Every day i am so grateful for the Internet Archive. it's so underrated. I wish the general public understood that every day that passes it becomes an increasingly crucial lode-bearing pillar of society and culture.

We have been told for so long that the internet is forever, and that's good advice in terms of thinking about what you want to put on the internet to represent you, but really bad advice in terms of history and research:

- no more Adobe Flash........
- so many broken links and expired domains and lost information........

I'm so excited to be taking this course and learning more about computers. i'm so fascinated by them and by the internet as a public space and I can't wait to understand even a tiny bit better how they work and how we might approach them as historians.
